# E-commerce Sales Analytics (SQL + Python)

An end-to-end analytics project on a messy, multi-table online-store dataset: **clean it, load it into a SQL database, answer business questions, and turn the results into recommendations.**

> **Data note:** the dataset is **synthetic**. It was generated by `scripts/01_generate_data.py` and deliberately contains real-world mess (duplicates, mixed date formats, casing, nulls, outliers, orphan rows) so the cleaning step is realistic. No real company data is used, and the numbers below describe this simulated store, not a real business.

## Business problem
A mid-size online store (ShopKart) wants to know where it makes and loses money:
1. Which categories and discount levels actually earn profit?
2. Which customers drive revenue, and who is about to churn?
3. Where does revenue leak (cancellations, returns, cash-on-delivery)?

## Tools
Python (pandas, matplotlib), SQL (SQLite; the same logic runs on Postgres / BigQuery), Excel for first-pass checks.

## Data cleaning (summary)
Full list in [`reports/data_quality_report.md`](reports/data_quality_report.md). Raw data is never edited; every fix is logged.
- 180 duplicate orders and 150 duplicate order lines dropped
- Order dates arrived in 4 different formats (16,688 rows parsed)
- 40 orphan orders (customer not in the customers table) removed and saved separately
- Invalid values (negative / fat-finger quantities, impossible delivery days) removed or set to null, **not** filled with averages

## Key findings
| Area | Finding |
|---|---|
| Overall | Revenue about ₹4.44 Cr, gross margin **34.0%**, average order value about **₹3,173** |
| Repeat buying | **61.7%** of buyers ordered more than once |
| Discounts | Margin falls from **40.8%** (no discount) to **6.1%** (35%+ discount). 633 order lines were sold at a loss |
| Festive season | Oct–Nov discounts averaged 19.6% vs 9.3% otherwise. Orders rose 38.9% over September, but gross profit **fell 4.7%** and margin dropped **8.4 points** |
| Categories | Electronics is the biggest (26.1% of revenue) at a thin **19.8%** margin. Beauty has the best margin (50.2%) |
| Customers (RFM) | "Champions" are **16.4%** of customers but **40.1%** of revenue |
| Retention | About **27.9%** of customers come back in month 1, **15.9%** by month 6 |
| Payments | Cash-on-delivery orders are cancelled **14.6%** of the time vs **6.6%** for other methods |

## Recommendations
1. **Cap festive discounts.** More orders but less profit means discounting above about 25% is buying volume at a loss. Test a smaller discount first.
2. **Protect the Champions.** 16% of customers give 40% of revenue, so a loyalty or early-access programme is cheap insurance.
3. **Reduce COD cancellations.** Offer a small prepaid incentive or a confirmation call on COD orders.
4. **Win back the "At Risk" group.** They spent a lot earlier (about ₹15.7K each) but have gone quiet for roughly a year.

## Limitations
- Synthetic data: patterns reflect how it was generated, so treat the findings as a worked example of the method.
- Correlation is not causation. For example, the discount and margin link is mechanical, but discount and repeat purchases would need an experiment (see the A/B test below).
- Missing product cost for 6 products was imputed from the category cost/MRP ratio (documented in the quality report).

## Extra analyses (done in notebooks)
- **A/B test** ("free delivery above ₹999" banner, synthetic data): conversion A 4.24% vs B 4.18%, difference -0.06 points, 95% CI -0.78 to +0.66 points, p = 0.869. **No detectable effect.** The test was underpowered: about 13,300 visitors per group would be needed to detect a 1-point difference, and each group had about 6,000. Slicing by device after the fact was treated as exploratory only.
- **Churn model** (logistic regression, features and label from separate time windows to avoid leakage): accuracy 0.79 vs 0.763 for the "everyone stays" baseline, AUC 0.825. Predicting churn is not the same as knowing who a discount would change; that needs an experiment.
